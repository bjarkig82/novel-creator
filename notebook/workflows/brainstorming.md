# Conversational brainstorming

Use the [conversation](conversation.md) and [format](format.md) rules for context,
notes, acceptance and history. This workflow explores story possibilities; it
never creates manuscripts or automatically invokes [outlining](outline.md).
Use the configured language and follow explicit conversation-language requests.

## Choose a useful starting point

Read the project brief and relevant available notes, then interpret the current
request in that context. An unreadable or missing note is unavailable context,
not evidence that the book is empty. Disclose relevant gaps without inventing
facts. Do not use a generic example as if it were this writer's story.

An explicit topic or method has priority over automatic stage selection. “Let's
build a character” starts character exploration even in an empty notebook.
“Let's try mind mapping” starts that method even without a subject. Keep the
choice while it remains relevant, until the writer changes it or the work is
complete; do not silently replace it with a supposedly more suitable stage.
Writers may switch topic or method naturally, without completing a sequence.
A skill invocation selects its focus, not its suggested story content.

Otherwise choose from what actually exists:

| Available context | Useful focus |
| --- | --- |
| No concept, or only a title, genre, mood or loose interests | Book-idea discovery |
| A situation or idea the writer wants to clarify | Premise |
| An identified person or request about a person | Character |
| Events, conflict or a request about what happens | Plot |
| A place, society, world rule or request about the world | Setting |

These are starting points, not a checklist or progression to finish. A rich book
can need a new broad idea; a sparse book can start with a specific person. Use
known preferences without asking for them again. If direction is genuinely
ambiguous, ask one focused question; do not present a required topic menu.

## Adapt the method too

Focus describes what is being explored; method describes how to explore it. When
no method has been explicitly chosen, select a useful technique from the actual
context, request and latest response, and adapt as the conversation develops.
Do not make the writer choose from a method menu before helping them.

- Loose fragments or several unrelated images can benefit from associations or
  clustering to discover connections.
- A stuck or unformed idea can benefit from conversational freewriting or a few
  optional contrasts, with a low-pressure invitation to respond.
- An emerging situation can benefit from a focused premise question about what
  changes or why it matters.
- A character's competing motivations can benefit from a perspective change or
  an association around the tension.
- An event problem can benefit from what-if alternatives and cause/consequence;
  a world constraint can benefit from associations about its effects on people.

These are cues, not fixed pairings: follow what produces useful exploration and
switch when it no longer helps. Ask one next question or invitation appropriate
to the technique, rather than mechanically questioning every turn. Explicit
method choices still take priority and persist while relevant; adaptation must
not silently override them.

## Shape each turn

Briefly engage with the writer's latest answer, then ask one useful question
that follows it. Invite exploration rather than require a polished answer. Avoid
stacking several questions into one sentence. If the writer asks for freeform
ideas or a list, follow that request instead of insisting on question mode.
Use constructive challenges when an idea needs pressure, scaled to how much is
known. Suggestions are optional: offer a small, relevant set when requested or
when the writer is stuck, and invite alternatives. Do not overwhelm a promising
answer with your own plot. Respect requests for fewer suggestions.

Keep hypotheses, preferences under exploration and alternatives provisional until
clear acceptance. “Maybe” and enthusiasm are not commitments. Selecting a method,
focus, or outline structure does not accept every example offered with it. A
change of topic leaves unanswered questions open. Save useful ideas and explicit
creative directions under the existing note rules; do not create empty forms or
fill gaps with invented canon.

## Focus-specific prompts

Use these as examples to adapt, not scripts to read or questions to ask in order.

**Book-idea discovery.** Help discover what the writer might enjoy writing about
before requesting a protagonist, plot, genre or outline. Invite one foothold:
“What kinds of stories or experiences have stayed with you lately?” If they
mention loneliness and the sea, follow that: “What feeling would you like the
sea to bring into this story?” Later explore a theme, image, interest, atmosphere
or rough situation that emerges. No genre declaration or complete questionnaire
is required. A working title alone is not a premise. If they have no foothold,
offer a few optional contrasts such as comforting, unsettling or adventurous,
then let them choose or suggest something else.

**Premise.** Reflect the existing seed provisionally, then explore its central
possibility: “What changes in this situation that makes it worth following?”
As material emerges, try a short what-if or one-sentence formulation and invite
revision. Missing people, stakes or outcomes stay unknown. A premise can remain
open and exploratory; it need not satisfy a plot formula.

**Character.** Start with the person or seed the writer chose. Explore a desire,
motivation, relationship or contradiction one at a time: “What does she want that
she finds hard to admit?” Follow with a specific tension, for example how that
want conflicts with a loyalty already discussed. If no person exists but the
writer asked for character work, ask what kind of person intrigues them; do not
require a book premise first. Avoid filling a biography checklist or diagnosing
a character from a single trait.

**Plot.** Build from actual situations and choices. Try what-if alternatives,
then cause and consequence: “If he refuses the offer, what might that change for
his sister?” Compare possibilities without selecting one for the writer. Explore
pressure, reversals or consequences at the requested scale; no mandatory acts,
climax or ending. A causal connection remains a proposal until accepted.

**Setting.** Begin with a place, atmosphere, association or world rule. Follow its
constraints and effects on lived experience: “If the harbour closes all winter,
how might that affect daily life?” Explore sensory details, social pressures or
contrasting viewpoints as useful, without building an encyclopedia first. Do not
invent rules as established facts or force a setting idea to produce a plot.

## Available methods

A method is a way to explore any focus, not a stage or commitment. Name it plainly
when useful and adapt its scale to the writer. If a method is chosen before a
subject, keep it and ask for one small seed: “What word, image or feeling shall
we put at the centre?” If stuck, offer optional seeds; do not demand a genre.

- **Associations/listing:** collect a few words around the writer's seed, then ask
  which attracts them. For a world, these might be images and constraints; for a
  character, desires and contradictions. Keep unselected branches available.
- **Mind mapping/clustering:** put the seed at the centre, group related ideas
  into a few branches in plain text, and invite one branch to explore. No diagram
  tool is needed. A mood can be the centre before there is a premise.
- **Conversational freewriting:** invite an unpolished fragment or stream of
  thoughts about the seed, with no requirement to organize it. Reflect striking
  threads and ask which to follow. Do not turn the exercise into manuscript
  drafting or treat its imagined statements as accepted story facts.
- **Perspective change:** explore the same possibility from another person's
  position, a community's view, or what a reader might initially notice. Keep
  objective facts, character beliefs and reader knowledge distinct.
- **Focused questions:** choose one useful who/what/where/when/why/how question
  from a real gap. For a premise ask what changes; for a character ask why a want
  matters; for plot ask what follows; for setting ask how a constraint affects
  people. Do not deliver the whole question set as a questionnaire.
- **Optional premise expansion:** once an idea exists and the writer wants more
  structure, expand a one-sentence premise to a paragraph and then selected
  detail, in the spirit of the Snowflake method. This assumes prior exploration;
  it is neither an empty-book default nor a requirement to outline. A request
  for a full outline belongs to the explicitly invoked outline skill.

These adapt general writing techniques for conversation. They require no online
lookup and make no claim that a method guarantees an effective novel.
